If you buy bitcoin postage for the same handful of products every week, international orders are where the time goes. Customs details get entered at checkout for every parcel going abroad, and typing them from memory means the same mug gets described three different ways in a month. A small reference table fixes that.

## What goes in the sheet

One row per SKU. It's your own spreadsheet that you copy from by hand, so shape the columns however you like:

| SKU | Customs description | Unit value | Origin | Packed weight |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MUG-11 | Ceramic coffee mug, 11 oz | (your figure) | (country made) | (weigh it boxed) |
| TEE-BLK-M | Men's cotton knit t-shirt | (your figure) | (country made) | (weigh it boxed) |

Habits that keep it useful:

- Say what the item is and what it's made of. "Ceramic coffee mug" tells a customs officer something. "Gift" or "merch" tells them nothing.
- Record the value the customs rules call for, keep receipts that back it up, and update the row when prices change.
- Note the country of manufacture, which may differ from where you ship.
- Weigh one packed unit. That number helps when you compare rates, too.
- Add a tariff code column if your destinations require one.

## Using it at checkout

On the [Bitcoin Shipping Labels site](https://www.bitcoinshippinglabels.com), you enter the addresses, packed weight and box dimensions, and international parcels also ask for customs information. Keep the sheet open in another tab and copy from it. For a mixed order, pull every item's row before you start.

The sheet records what you meant to declare. Before you finish checkout, screenshot the customs details you actually entered and file it with the order ID. When a buyer abroad asks why a parcel is sitting in customs, that saved record is what you check.
